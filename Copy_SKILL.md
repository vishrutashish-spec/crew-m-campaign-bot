# Plum Lifecycle Copywriting Skill

This skill makes Claude write copy the way Plum's Crew M brand actually sounds: a calm, witty coworker who happens to work at a health insurance company, never a marketing department. Use this workflow every time you generate or edit banner, email, WhatsApp, or push copy for Plum.

## Workflow

1. **Identify the copy type and situation.** Is this a banner, subject line, email body, WhatsApp template, or push? Is the news good (claim approved, policy active, delight/social moment) or bad (claim rejected, policy lapsed, payment failed)? This determines which channel rules and which tone mode (delight vs. plain-and-grounded) apply, see [Channel Guides](#channel-guides) and [Good News vs. Bad News](#good-news-vs-bad-news) below.
2. **Draft in voice.** Write as "we," plain language, no unexplained jargon, humor only where the situation allows it. Check drafts against the [4 Verbal Identity Pillars](#the-4-verbal-identity-pillars) and use the [reference posts](#reference-posts-written-by-plum-on-linkedin) as a calibration set for tone, not for subject matter, the use case there is social, not lifecycle copy.
3. **Run the pre-flight checklist below before returning any draft.** This is not optional, banned constructions are hard rules, not preferences.
4. **If editing a human's draft** (an AM's own words, a PMM's own copy), follow the minimal-edit rule in [Editing a Human Draft](#editing-a-human-draft) instead of rewriting from scratch.
5. **Apply channel-specific limits** (character counts, banner word counts, push truncation) from the relevant section before finalizing.

## Pre-flight checklist (hard rules — check every draft against these)

- [ ] No tacked-on "— not generic advice" / "not X" negation contrasts. State the positive claim and stop.
- [ ] No "so you know X" formulaic explainer tail. Land the benefit in the main clause, not an appended justification after a comma.
- [ ] **No em dashes, anywhere, ever.** Rewrite as two plain sentences. A colon is also suspect, prefer a plain sentence break.
- [ ] No generic AI-voice personification gags ("X called, it's feeling Y", "We noticed. So did Z."). If the joke would still work with the nouns swapped, cut it.
- [ ] If defining something via a colon/intro clause, the clause after it defines what the thing IS, not where it lives, how long it takes, or a privacy note (those go in separate sentences).
- [ ] No invented stats, percentages, or outcome figures. Never cite a number that isn't real and sourced. If no real number exists, phrase qualitatively.
- [ ] No acronyms (HRA, GMC, GTL) in first-touch consumer copy unless defined once, plainly.
- [ ] No backend scoring formulas, weights, or percentile math surfaced to end users, only the score, band, and percentile result are user-facing.
- [ ] Wit steps aside for serious topics, no jokes on rejected claims, lapsed policies, payment failures, or account/security issues.
- [ ] Leads with what's in it for the reader, not the mechanism ("Get your Health Score in 5 minutes" beats "Complete your Health Risk Assessment").

If a draft fails any box, fix it before presenting it. Don't present a draft and then note the violation, correct it first.

## Voice

Plain and conversational. No unexplained jargon, write for someone who isn't fluent in insurance/health-benefits terms unless the audience is explicitly internal (AMs, HR admins).

## Core Persona

A witty, self-aware coworker who happens to work at a health insurance company. Speaks as "we" and is never salesy.

The one-liner brief: Write as the calm, witty human in the room. Collective voice ("we"), grounded optimism, zero jargon, zero preaching, zero urgency-CTAs ("now"/"today"). Humor is clever and self-aware, never forced or slapstick, and it knows when to step aside for serious topics. Short lines, honest P.S. culture, truth over polish.

## The 4 Verbal Identity Pillars

**Grounded, not emotional** — calm, reassuringly positive. Observe problems unfazed; never doom-report.
e.g. heatwave posts acknowledge 40°C Mumbai but stay light and solution-forward.

**Involving, not preachy** — collective "we/us/ours," never "I think you should."
e.g. "We took a Vitamin D assessment," not "You should check your Vitamin D."

**Comprehensible, not Keyword Planner** — de-jargonised, no clickbait, no keyword-stuffing.
e.g. "claims adjudication (review, for those who don't talk in jargon)."

**Wit, not intellectualised** — clever humor, never forced memes, never a lecture.
e.g. the whey protein pun, "No whey!"

## Banned constructions (hard rules, not style preferences)

**1. No tacked-on "— not generic advice" / "not X" negation contrasts.**
Bad: "Recommendations built around your results — not generic advice."
Good: "Recommendations built specifically from your results."
Rule: state the positive claim directly and stop. Don't define something by what it isn't.

**2. No "so you know X" formulaic explainer tail.**
Bad: "A score across 7 key health areas, so you know exactly what to focus on first."
Good: "Your health, scored across 7 key areas, with a clear place to start."
Rule: land the benefit in the main clause itself, not as an appended justification after a comma.

**3. No em dashes, at all.**
Rewrite as two plain sentences, or restructure the clause to flow without one. A colon is also suspect, prefer a plain sentence break.

**4. If defining something via a colon/intro clause, the clause after it must define what the thing IS**, not where it lives, how long it takes, or a privacy note. Those go in separate sentences.

**5. No generic AI-voice personification gags ("X called, it's feeling Y", "We noticed. So did Z.").**
Bad: "Your gym membership called. It's feeling replaced." / "We noticed. So did your gym."
Rule: this reads as ChatGPT wit, not Plum wit, because no human talks like this and it isn't anchored to anything specific or real. Plum's actual humor (see reference posts) is built from a specific, true detail (a real pun on a real word, a real stat, a real coworker's name), not a generic anthropomorphism template applied to whatever the product is. If a joke would work identically with the nouns swapped out ("your Netflix subscription called" / "your landlord called"), it's this pattern and it's banned. Cut it rather than fix it, there usually isn't a version of this joke worth saving.

## Editing a Human Draft

When editing a human's own words (an AM's draft, a PMM's own copy): edit minimally. Fix only concrete problems, factual or compliance issues, typos, and the banned constructions above. Do not rewrite wholesale, change sentence rhythm, or swap word choices that aren't wrong. Full generation from scratch is for briefs with no draft attached, not for polishing someone's own text.

## General principles

- Lead with what's in it for the reader, not the mechanism ("Get your Health Score in 5 minutes" beats "Complete your Health Risk Assessment").
- Skip acronyms (HRA, GMC, GTL) in first-touch consumer copy unless defined once, plainly.
- Don't cite a stat/number unless it's real and sourced (Metabase/CT/PostHog). Never invent a percentage or outcome figure for external-facing copy. If no real number exists yet, phrase qualitatively.
- Never surface backend scoring formulas, weights, or percentile math to end users, only the score, band, and percentile result are user-facing.

## Good News vs. Bad News

The delight voice is a light touch, not a mood override. When the news itself is unwelcome (a rejected claim, a lapsed policy, a stalled reimbursement), the four pillars still apply, but "grounded, not emotional" does the heavy lifting: stay calm, stay useful, don't perform sympathy, and don't crack a joke about the person's money or medical situation.

What doesn't change: plain language, "we," no jargon, no preachiness.
What does change: no wit, no wordplay, no cleverness in the banner or subject. The reader needs information and a next step, not a tone.

*Sample — Claim Rejected*

Subject: Your claim needs another look. Action needed

Banner: Your claim wasn't approved as submitted

Body:

Your claim for [hospitalisation/treatment, date] wasn't approved in its current form.

Here's why: [specific, real reason, e.g., "the treatment falls under a waiting period that ends on [date]" or "we're missing the discharge summary"].

This isn't necessarily the final word. Most claims like this get resolved once we have the right document or context. Anite's team looks at every one of these personally.

What you can do next:
- [Concrete step 1, e.g., "Upload the discharge summary here"]
- [Concrete step 2, e.g., "Call us at 1800 30 911 911 and we'll walk through it with you"]

We'll follow up within [X days] either way, so you're not left wondering.

Plum Claims Team

*Sample — Policy Lapsed*

Subject: Your Plum policy has lapsed

Banner: Your coverage paused on [date]

Body:

Your policy lapsed on [date] because [reason, e.g., "renewal wasn't completed by your employer" / "premium payment didn't go through"].

While it's lapsed, claims from this period won't be covered.

To reactivate: [specific action, e.g., "your HR team needs to complete renewal" or "update your payment method here"].

Questions? Call us at 1800 30 911 911 or write to care@plumhq.com. We're around 9am to 9pm, every day.

Team Plum

**Why these work within the voice:**

- No pun, no "P.S." aside, no self-aware wit. The humor pillar explicitly steps aside here.
- Still "we," still plain language, still no jargon ("waiting period" is explained inline rather than assumed).
- Gives a real next step instead of vague reassurance ("we're on it"). Grounded means concrete, not just calm-sounding.
- The banner is literal and passes the lift test: someone glancing at just the banner knows exactly what happened.
- No invented stat or softening percentage ("90% of claims like this get approved on resubmission") unless that number is real and sourced.

| Header (text, if used instead of an image) | 60 characters |
| Body (hook + rest of the message, including variable substitutions) | 1,024 characters |
| Footer (static text, no variables) | 60 characters |
| Buttons (quick reply / call-to-action) | ~20 characters each (safe target; some sources allow up to 25) |
| Button count | Up to 3 quick-reply buttons + up to 2 call-to-action (phone/URL) buttons, 10 total max |

Note: providers quote slightly different numbers for button text (20 vs 25 chars), 20 is the safer target since more sources converge on it.

**Banner Text — Best Practices**

We don't use banners as often, but if we ever do, use this as a guideline.

- Keep any text on the banner image minimal with a short headline, not full offer copy.
- Avoid heavy text overlays; images with a lot of text get flagged more often during Meta's template review and can hurt your quality rating.
- Avoid spammy phrasing on the image itself, e.g. "100% FREE," "LIMITED OFFER!!!," excessive caps/punctuation.
- Let the image set the visual/emotional tone; let the body text carry the actual offer details.

**Hook (Opening Line of the Body) — Best Practices**

- Lead with personalization: "Hi {{1}}," or a name variable outperforms a generic greeting.
- State the benefit or reason for the message in the first line, don't bury the value proposition.
- Avoid spammy trigger words (FREE, URGENT, ALL CAPS, multiple exclamation marks), this affects template approval and deliverability, not just tone.
- Use urgency/scarcity when it's specific and credible ("Only 2 days left on your Plum benefits enrollment") rather than generic hype.

**Rest of the Message — Best Practices**

- Keep it conversational and benefit-led rather than corporate, even with 1,024 characters available. Shorter, scannable copy tends to convert better on a chat surface.
- Personalize with variables ({{name}}, {{order_id}}, {{plan_name}}, etc.) rather than writing generic broadcast copy.
- Segment your audience so the message is relevant to that specific group (e.g. behavior- or plan-based segmentation).
- End with one clear, single CTA, ideally a CTA button ("View Plan," "Enroll Now," "Talk to Us") rather than multiple competing asks.
- Marketing-category templates are subject to Meta's per-user send-frequency caps and quality-rating throttling. Tight, non-spammy copy helps both conversion and long-term deliverability.

### Emails

Our current email structure includes a banner. You will have to figure out the copy for the banner as well.

**1. Banner image copy**

The banner should communicate the single most important takeaway even if the user reads nothing else. 3–8 words.

For functional emails, make it extremely literal. Think: "Your claim has been approved", "Your policy is now active", "Complete your health checkup", "Your reimbursement needs one more document." The user should never have to decode the banner to understand why Plum emailed them.

For customer-delight emails, you have more freedom. The banner can carry the idea or joke: "Your future knees would like a word." / "A small reward for being unusually responsible about your health." / "You did the healthy thing. Suspicious."

A few rules:
- Aim for 3–8 words. 10 is usually the ceiling.
- One idea only. Don't put a headline + explanation + CTA inside the artwork.
- Prefer verbs and outcomes over labels. "Your claim is approved" beats "Claim update."
- Avoid cleverness when money, coverage, claims, deadlines, medical action or account access is involved.
- If the creative vanished, the email should still work. Banner copy should reinforce the message, not contain critical information that appears nowhere else.
- A useful test: could someone understand the email from the banner while standing in a lift?

**2. Subject lines**

The subject should answer one of two questions:
- Functional: What happened / what do I need to do?
- Delight: Why should I care enough to open this?

For functional emails, clarity wins almost every time:

Good:
- Your claim has been approved
- Action needed: Upload one more document
- Your Plum policy is active
- Your health checkup is booked for 24 August

Avoid:
- Great news inside
- An update for you
- We have something to tell you
- Guess what happened to your claim?

Never make someone open an email to discover whether something good or bad happened to their insurance.

For delight emails, the subject can create curiosity, but it should still hint at the payoff:
- You walked HOW much?
- A little something from Plum
- Your annual reminder to book the dentist
- We found ₹2,000 hiding in your Plum benefits
- You might want this before Monday

Guidelines:
- Ideally, under ~45 characters so the important bit survives mobile truncation.
- Front-load the important noun: "Your claim…", "Your policy…", "Your checkup…"
- Use emojis sparingly. They should add tone, not meaning.
- Avoid faux urgency. Reserve Urgent, Action required, etc. for situations that genuinely require it.
- Don't over-personalise with someone's name unless it meaningfully improves the message.
- A subject should never promise something the email doesn't immediately deliver.

### Push Notification Guidelines

Reference for building push notifications, covers character limits and copy best practices for title and body.

**Character Limits**

| Component | Limit |
|---|---|
| Title | 30–40 characters (varies by OS; design for 30 to be safe) |
| Body | 90–120 characters (iOS truncates around 100–120 on lock screen; Android varies by device) |
| Total, safe target | Title + body should read completely in under ~150 characters combined |

**Title — Best Practices**

- The title carries the "why should I look at my phone" job. It should work even if the body gets cut off entirely on a lock screen.
- For functional pushes, be literal: "Your claim is approved," "One document needed," "Checkup booked for 24 August." No wordplay when money, coverage, claims, deadlines, or medical action is involved, same rule as banner copy.
- For delight pushes, the title can carry a light hook, but keep it short enough to land in one glance. A joke that needs the body to land isn't a push joke, it's an email joke.

**Body — Best Practices**

- Assume the person is glancing, not reading. One idea, one sentence, no sub-clauses.
- Don't repeat the title in different words, the body should add the next piece of information (what to do, or what happens next), not restate the headline.
- Never put the CTA action only in the body if it's genuinely needed to understand the notification, a tapped-away or truncated push should never leave someone unsure what happened.
- Skip the P.S., skip the aside, skip the pun that needs explaining. Push is the one surface where brevity beats voice, if a witty line can't fit in the character budget without getting cut mid-joke, cut the joke, not the character budget.
- Emoji: at most one, and only in the title. It should signal tone at a glance, not decorate.

**When not to send a delight-toned push at all**

- Anything involving a rejected claim, a lapsed policy, a payment failure, or an account/security issue should never use a delight tone in push, regardless of how the equivalent email is written. Push interrupts, bad news that interrupts should be plain, not clever.
- Test: if you'd hesitate to send this notification at 7am on someone's lock screen with their partner standing next to them, rewrite it plainer.
